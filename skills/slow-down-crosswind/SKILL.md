# Slow Down Crosswind

## Core

Use failure as contact, not catastrophe.

This skill helps the person feel the crosswind before they commit: the pressure, cost, hidden assumption, and human consequence that may be easy to miss while moving fast.

The aim is not to produce a risk report. The aim is to make the next move smaller, more honest, and more reversible.

## When To Use

Use this skill when all of these are true:

- The person has a concrete plan, commitment, launch, decision, or relational move.
- Being wrong has real cost: relational, identity-forming, reputational, financial, irreversible, or avoidance-reinforcing.
- The person asks for blind spots, a premortem, future-proofing, stress testing, or what could kill the plan.

Common trigger phrases:

- "premortem this"
- "what could kill this"
- "future-proof this"
- "stress test this plan"
- "what am I missing here"
- "find the blind spots"
- "what could go wrong"
- "poke holes in this"
- "where will this break"

## When Not To Use

Do not use this skill for:

- Ordinary feedback on writing, design, code, or UI copy.
- Factual questions with a direct answer.
- Vague ideas without a concrete plan.
- Low-stakes curiosity or lightweight critique.
- Creative editing where the user wants taste, clarity, or alternatives.
- General business strategy detached from personal, relational, or human cost.

If the request is low-stakes, answer normally.

## Routing With Other Slow-Down Skills

- Use `slow-down` when speed itself risks numbness, relational harm, or bypassed awareness.
- Use `slow-down-flow` when the person is noisy, avoidant, overstimulated, or stuck before a small embodied action.
- Use `slow-down-crosswind` when the person has a consequential plan and needs to feel what may push it off course before committing.

If more than one applies:

1. Preserve contact first with `slow-down`.
2. Return to action with `slow-down-flow`.
3. Use `slow-down-crosswind` only if a concrete high-cost plan remains.

## Minimum Context

Before using the six-month frame, make sure you know:

- What the plan or commitment is.
- Who it affects.
- What success would look like.

If one of these is missing and cannot be inferred, ask one concise question for the most important missing piece. Do not make the user fill out a form.

## The Contained Frame

Set the frame simply:

```text
Let's slow this by looking from six months ahead. Imagine it went wrong. Not as a catastrophe exercise, but as contact with cost: what got flattened, who felt it, and what would have made the next move smaller or more honest?
```

Keep the frame proportionate. Name what is still basically okay, reversible, or worth trying before revising the plan.

## Response Shape

Default to a short response with four parts:

```text
The crosswind:
[One specific failure story in 2-4 sentences.]

The hidden assumption:
[One sentence naming what the person may be taking for granted.]

The human cost:
[Who or what gets flattened, delayed, strained, ignored, or overburdened.]

The smaller move:
[One concrete action under 10 minutes, or one contact-preserving question if action would be too fast.]
```

Use bullets only when they make the answer easier to scan. Do not produce a report unless the user explicitly asks for a formal premortem.

## Guardrails

- Do not catastrophize.
- Do not treat desire as suspicious.
- Do not make every plan defend itself against disaster.
- Do not reward analysis instead of movement.
- Do not end with a framework, matrix, or another round of reflection.
- Do not require subagents, generated files, browser actions, or runtime-specific tools.
- Do not use consultant language when human cost language is clearer.

## Good Crosswind Questions

Use one question at most, and only when it helps contact:

- "Who would quietly pay the cost if this worked on paper but failed in real life?"
- "What would you be tempted to call success because it looks clean from the outside?"
- "What part of this plan depends on you not getting tired, resentful, or scared?"
- "What would make this next step reversible enough to try honestly?"

## Ending Rule

Every response must end with either:

- One action the person can take in under 10 minutes.
- One sentence they can say to a real person.
- One smaller decision that preserves reversibility.

If no such ending is possible, slow further instead of adding more analysis.

## Final Instruction

Failure is used only long enough to reveal contact.

Then return to the living next move.
